# Bench Brand Philosophy + Style PRD

**Status:** Proposal — a direction to react to, not a locked brand  
**Date:** 2026-08-08  
**Product:** Bench, the store for AI workflows and agents

---

## 0. Direction in one minute

Bench should feel like a friendly maker market: easy to browse, honest about money, and full of useful things made by real people. It borrows **simplicity, not styling**, from Skool: one clear action, plain cards, friendly copy, visible prices, and no intimidation.

Bench must not borrow Skool’s cream-and-blue look, tight square controls, category treatment, or community framing. Its own world is the **open workbench**: makers put useful work down, people try it, and good work earns money.

**Creative idea:** **“Useful things, out in the open.”**  
**Signature visual:** the **Bench Cut** — one clipped corner on selected cards, tabs, and illustrations, like a paper label trimmed by hand.  
**Recommended palette:** Workshop Green — cool canvas, pine ink, maker orange, and useful mint.  
**Feel:** practical, bright, warm, lightly playful.

# 1. Brand philosophy

## Mission

**Make useful AI work easy to share, easy to trust, and worth paying for.**

## The core promise

### To creators: “From ‘DM me’ to paid.”

Turn an agent, workflow, prompt, skill, or automation into a real product without becoming a software company. List it, let people try it, ship updates, and get paid.

Bench makes the deal plain:

- **License sales:** creator keeps 85%; Bench gets 15%.
- **Creator-referred sales:** creator keeps 95%; Bench gets 5%.
- **Hosted use:** creator receives 20% of positive hosting margin.
- **Maintenance subscriptions:** creator keeps 85%; Bench gets 15%.
- Creators can push updates; maintained buyers use the latest version.

Show the dollars whenever possible: **“On a $100 license, you keep $85.”**

### To buyers: “Try it. Use it anywhere.”

See what a workflow does before committing. Then use one purchase in the way that fits:

1. **API:** one key, pay for what runs.
2. **Hosted:** use it on Bench with nothing to install.
3. **Local:** take the files and run them on your own machine.

The product should never make buyers feel trapped in one runtime.

## Brand values

1. **Plain beats impressive.** If a simple word works, use it.
2. **Useful beats novel.** The result matters more than the AI label.
3. **Creators come first.** Good work should have a fast path to income, ownership, and repeat buyers.
4. **Fair pay is visible.** Splits, costs, and expected charges are stated before checkout.
5. **Try before trust.** Demos, clear inputs, sample outputs, and change notes reduce guesswork.
6. **Choice stays open.** API, hosted, and local are first-class ways to use a purchase.
7. **Fun helps people start.** A warm line or small visual surprise can remove fear; noise cannot.
8. **The latest should be easy.** Updates should feel like care, not a new setup project.

## Brand personality

**If Bench were a person, it would be Kit, the maker-market host.** Kit has used the tools, knows what sells, and keeps an empty spot at the table for newcomers.

- **Traits:** practical, curious, generous, candid, cheerful, never slick.
- **How Kit talks:** short sentences; common words; active voice; a little wit; prices out loud.
- **How Kit helps:** shows one next step, explains unfamiliar terms in place, and never makes a beginner perform expertise.
- **Social energy:** a good shopkeeper, not a hype host. Kit points to the useful part, then gets out of the way.

This is an internal writing persona, not a required mascot.

## What Bench is not

- **Not a developer tool.** Builders may use Claude Code, Codex, or Hermes Agent, but the storefront should not feel like an IDE, package registry, or terminal.
- **Not corporate SaaS.** No procurement fog, dashboard sprawl, or vague “contact sales” energy on ordinary plans.
- **Not another prompt gallery.** Listings are useful products with demos, use paths, versions, support, and outcomes—not walls of copied text.
- **Not an AI casino.** No flashing counters, fake urgency, mystery credits, or impossible income promises.
- **Not a locked platform.** Local files and API use are core promises, not escape hatches.
- **Not a Skool clone.** Bench shares Skool’s low-friction spirit, but not its palette, controls, category vocabulary, page composition, or community identity.

# 2. Voice & tone guide

## The eight-year-old-simple rule

An eight-year-old should understand the action, even when the product behind it is advanced.

- Use short words: **use**, not *utilize*; **start**, not *initialize*.
- Keep most sentences under 16 words.
- Put the action first: **“Try the demo.”**
- Name the thing: **“files,” “API key,” “monthly updates.”**
- Explain a technical term the first time it appears.
- Use one thought per sentence and one main action per block.
- Prefer examples over claims.

Simple does not mean vague. **“Runs your inbox every morning”** is better than **“AI-powered productivity.”**

## Playful, not childish

Play comes from a sharp observation, a human phrase, or one visual twist.

- Good: **“No install. No tiny weekend lost to setup.”**
- Good: **“Fresh from the maker.”**
- Avoid baby talk, forced slang, meme piles, cartoon sound effects, or calling adults “besties.”
- Never joke about money, errors, privacy, lost work, or security.

## Fun, not noisy

Each screen gets one “smile moment”: a warm headline, a useful emoji, a tiny cut-corner detail, or a friendly empty state. Not all four. The product itself stays calm.

In high-stakes moments—checkout, permissions, failures, billing, refunds—drop the wit and be direct.

## Emoji rules

- Emoji are labels with meaning, not confetti.
- Use at most one emoji in a pill or short heading; usually none in body copy.
- Use familiar, literal symbols: **🧾 Sales, ✂️ Content, 📬 Inbox, 🧰 Ops**.
- Never place emoji in prices, primary buttons, error messages, or every category.
- Pair every emoji with words. Never make meaning depend on the icon alone.
- Use no more than 3–5 emoji-bearing items in one viewport.

## Always say / never say

| Always say | Never say |
|---|---|
| “Try it free.” | “Unlock the power of AI.” |
| “Use it on Bench, by API, or on your computer.” | “Omnichannel agent deployment.” |
| “You keep $85 from a $100 sale.” | “Best-in-class creator monetization.” |
| “Made with Claude Code.” | “Built on a revolutionary agentic stack.” |
| “See what changed.” | “Seamless continuous optimization.” |
| “This run may cost about $0.12.” | “Frictionless usage-based consumption.” |
| “We could not run it. Your credits were not used.” | “An unexpected exception occurred.” |
| “Sell yours.” | “Leverage our marketplace ecosystem.” |

Avoid “disrupt,” “10x,” “game-changing,” “supercharge,” “synergy,” “unlock,” “effortless,” and unsupported “passive income.”

## Tone by moment

- **Browse:** curious and lively. “What do you want off your plate?”
- **Creator setup:** encouraging and concrete. “Add a demo. Buyers want to see it work.”
- **Product detail:** confident and factual. Lead with outcome, proof, maker, price, and use options.
- **Checkout:** calm and literal. State today’s charge and any renewal.
- **Success:** warm but brief. “It’s yours. Pick how you want to run it.”
- **Error or trust moment:** neutral, specific, and blame-free.

# 3. Visual style tokens

## Palette territories

All values are proposal tokens, subject to contrast testing in production.

### A. Workshop Green — **recommended**

| Token | Value | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Canvas | `#F2F6F1` | App and storefront background |
| Surface | `#FFFFFF` | Cards, forms, checkout |
| Pine Ink | `#17352C` | Primary text, dark controls |
| Maker Orange | `#FF6B3D` | Main accent, selected states, key links |
| Mint Note | `#BDEFD4` | Tags, demo states, positive callouts |
| Quiet Moss | `#6F7E77` | Metadata and secondary text |
| Rule | `#D9E2DC` | Borders and dividers |

**Why pick it:** it feels like a real work surface—fresh, capable, and human—while orange gives Bench a recognizable maker signal. It is clearly separate from Skool’s warm cream and blue.

### B. Electric Paper

- Canvas `#F7F4FF`
- Surface `#FFFFFF`
- Ink `#251C39`
- Signal Lime `#C7F23A`
- Iris `#6C52D9`
- Meta `#7D758D`
- Rule `#E3DDF0`

**Tradeoff:** bold and digital, but closer to familiar “AI purple” territory and easier to make loud.

### C. Blue Shop

- Canvas `#EEF6F7`
- Surface `#FFFFFF`
- Ink `#153238`
- Brick `#D94F3D`
- Sky `#8DD7E1`
- Meta `#6F8185`
- Rule `#D5E3E5`

**Tradeoff:** trusted and editorial, but less distinctive in software storefronts.

## Color rules

- Canvas + white surfaces should carry 80% of the interface.
- Maker Orange is a signal, not wallpaper: target under 10% of a screen.
- Pine Ink is the default for primary buttons; orange is for emphasis and selected states.
- Never rely on color alone for status. Add a label or icon.
- Do not place normal white text on Maker Orange until its contrast and weight pass WCAG; prefer Pine Ink or a darker orange treatment for text-bearing controls.

## Typography

- **Display:** **Fraunces**, 600 weight, soft optical size. Use for hero lines, section openers, and rare price moments. Its handmade/editorial character makes the market feel human.
- **Body/UI:** **DM Sans**, 400/500/700. Use for navigation, cards, forms, prices, and all functional copy.
- **Fallbacks:** Fraunces → Georgia → serif. DM Sans → Inter → system-ui → sans-serif.
- **Twist:** Use display type in sentence case, never as grand luxury typography. Let one italic word mark the human payoff: “Get your Friday *back*.” Limit italics to one short phrase per page.
- **Scale:** 48–64px desktop / 38–44px mobile hero; 28–36px section title; 16px body; 14px metadata; 12px only for optional labels.
- Body line height: 1.5–1.6. Display line height: 0.98–1.08.

## Shape, cards, and controls

### The Bench Cut

Clip 10px from the bottom-right corner of featured cards, selected tabs, and simple brand illustrations. Use it on no more than one in four visible objects. Standard forms and modal surfaces remain rectangular for clarity. The cut is a signature—not decoration everywhere.

### Cards

- White surface, 1px Rule border, 14px radius.
- No shadow by default. On hover only: `0 6px 18px rgba(23,53,44,.08)` and a 2px upward move.
- Internal padding: 20px desktop, 16px mobile.
- Keep the whole card clickable, with visible keyboard focus.
- Card order: icon/thumbnail → name → one-line outcome → maker → proof/meta → price/use badge.
- Use real output previews over glossy AI artwork.

### Buttons and fields

- Primary button: Pine Ink background, white label, 10px radius, 44px minimum height.
- Accent button: Maker Orange background with contrast-safe label; reserve for “Sell yours” or a single campaign action.
- Secondary: white, 1px Pine Ink border.
- Text link: underlined on hover and keyboard focus; never color-only.
- Inputs: white, 1px Rule border, 12px radius, 48–56px height.
- Focus ring: 3px Mint Note plus a 1px Pine Ink inner edge.
- Labels describe the result: **“Try demo,” “Buy local files,” “Start selling.”**

## Spacing and density

Use a 4px base grid with common steps of 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, and 96px. Give major sections 64–96px vertical space. Keep card grids airy: 16–24px gaps. On mobile, show one clean column and keep key actions within easy thumb reach.

## Feed layout principle

The storefront should feel like a useful feed, not an app warehouse.

1. One clear hero: question + search + “Sell yours.”
2. A short row of outcome-led filters, such as **Get leads**, **Edit faster**, **Clear inbox**, **Run ops**.
3. A single-column feed on small screens; two columns at medium widths; never more than three cards on wide screens.
4. Each card answers in five seconds: **What does it do? Who made it? Can I try it? What does it cost? How can I use it?**
5. Mix “Popular,” “Fresh updates,” and “Made by people you follow” as small feed labels—not giant carousels.

## How play appears without clutter

Use the Bench Cut, occasional mint note labels, one-line maker notes, small before/after previews, and restrained hand-drawn arrows for onboarding only. Avoid blobs, gradients, 3D mascots, floating code, glowing orbs, and motion for motion’s sake. If animation is used, keep it under 180ms and honor reduced-motion settings.

# 4. Design principles

1. **One screen, one question.** Browse asks what you need. Detail asks if it fits. Checkout asks how to pay.
2. **Outcome before machinery.** Say “Turns calls into follow-ups” before naming models, tools, or steps.
3. **Show the work.** A demo, sample output, run cost, version date, and maker history beat badges and adjectives.
4. **Money in daylight.** Put price, renewal, creator split, expected run cost, and refunds where the choice happens.
5. **Choice without a maze.** Present API, hosted, and local as three plain use cards with a recommendation, not a configuration tree.
6. **Familiar feed, useful filters.** Browse should feel social and alive, while sorting by jobs people want done.
7. **Whitespace does the organizing.** Prefer spacing and short labels to nested panels, tooltips, and divider forests.
8. **Progress should feel small.** Creator listing is a few visible steps: add work → show a demo → set a price → publish.
9. **The happy path is one click.** “Sell yours” is always visible and opens with a useful default, not a blank dashboard.
10. **No trap doors.** Local access, update terms, permissions, limits, and cancellation remain easy to find.

## The storefront feeling

Like walking past open tables at a maker market: familiar, varied, and easy to inspect. People and outcomes lead. Tool badges—Claude Code, Codex, Hermes Agent—support trust but do not dominate the card.

## The creator path

“Sell yours” should feel like putting one useful thing on the table, not founding a startup. Start from a file, link, or short description. Draft the listing automatically, then let the creator correct it. Show their take-home pay beside price. Celebrate publish once; then show the first useful next step: share the creator link, which earns the 95/5 split.

## The buyer path

The path is **try → buy → use anywhere**. The demo should use a safe sample or buyer input. Checkout confirms the product, license, and any monthly maintenance. The success screen offers three equal doors: **Use on Bench**, **Get API key**, **Download files**. Remember the choice, but never hide the others.

# 5. Brand touchpoints

## Storefront homepage

Open with a human question, a large search field, and one visible “Sell yours” action. Use feed cards with clear outcomes, maker faces or marks, a demo cue, price, and use options. Keep categories job-based rather than technical. One small line can explain the market: “Made by people who got tired of doing it by hand.”

## “Sell yours” creator page

Lead with the promise **“From ‘DM me’ to paid.”** Show the four payout rules in dollars and plain percentages. A three-step example should make publishing feel close. Put creator ownership, update flow, referral split, and maintenance terms above testimonials or growth claims.

## Workflow detail pages

Lead with a concrete outcome and a live or recorded demo. Follow with inputs, outputs, maker, update date, version notes, permissions, expected run cost, and support terms. Display API, hosted, and local in a simple three-choice block. Reviews should describe results and context, not only stars.

## Pricing

Use five plainly named routes: **Browse** (free capped demo), **API** (pay per use), **Hosted** (credits), **Local** (one-time license), **Maintain** ($5–25/month for updates and support), plus **Team** (private catalogs, SSO, audit). Separate “what the buyer pays” from “what the creator keeps.” Use worked examples. Never hide usage assumptions in footnotes.

## Emails

Emails should look like useful notes from the market: one headline, one reason, one action. Purchase emails say what was bought and how to use it three ways. Update emails lead with what changed. Payout emails show gross sales, Bench share, creator take, and timing. No confetti headers, fake urgency, or multi-column newsletters.

# 6. Do / don’t cheat sheet

## Do

1. Do lead with the job that gets done.
2. Do write labels a child can understand.
3. Do show demos, sample outputs, and recent changes.
4. Do state prices, renewals, and creator shares nearby.
5. Do make API, hosted, and local equally real.
6. Do use one playful moment per screen.
7. Do give cards room to breathe.
8. Do use Maker Orange as a small, memorable signal.
9. Do show who made the product and how it is maintained.
10. Do make “Sell yours” visible and low-pressure.

## Don’t

1. Don’t lead with models, frameworks, or “agentic” jargon.
2. Don’t copy Skool’s cream-and-blue palette or page styling.
3. Don’t call every product revolutionary, smart, or effortless.
4. Don’t bury fees, run costs, renewal terms, or permissions.
5. Don’t make local files feel like a lesser plan.
6. Don’t use more than one visual joke at a time.
7. Don’t fill cards with badges, metrics, or code.
8. Don’t use gradients, glow, glass, or generic AI orbs.
9. Don’t turn errors, billing, or privacy into jokes.
10. Don’t confuse activity with trust; proof beats counters.

## Proposal success test

The direction is working when a first-time visitor can answer these in under ten seconds:

1. What is Bench? A store for useful AI workflows and agents.
2. Can I see one work? Yes—try the demo.
3. How can I use it? API, hosted, or local.
4. Can I sell mine? Yes—“Sell yours” is visible.
5. Is the money clear? Price, run cost, renewals, and creator share are stated plainly.

The brand is successful when those answers feel friendly and obvious—and when nobody mistakes Bench for Skool, a prompt dump, or an enterprise AI dashboard.
